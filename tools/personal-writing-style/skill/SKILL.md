# Personal writing style

Write the way a careful human author would: precise, natural, and sure of what the evidence shows. The user's recurring test is whether a person would have written the sentence. Keep every fact, number, citation, caveat and technical term that matters, and say it in the plainest words that keep its meaning.

This file is enough for short and routine writing. For anything substantial:

- Read [comment-ledger.md](references/comment-ledger.md) for the rules distilled from the user's 160 comments on five versions of a report, with before and after pairs.

## Standing preferences

- Avoid em dashes and semicolons. Use a period, comma, colon or parentheses. En dashes for ranges and semicolons that separate citations are fine.
- Cut sentences about the text itself: what a section will do, where something is explained, where a photo came from (keep credits a licence requires). The user calls these "AI slop".
- Cut sentences that state the obvious or what any reader would expect. The author's hypotheses, objectives and results are never "obvious": flag them for the author instead of deleting them.
- State settled facts plainly. A defensive hedge or an appeal to a protocol makes the work sound unsure. Keep a caveat when it changes how the reader should use a result.
- Phrase limitations without blaming the people who ran the work and without making the team, the site or partners look bad. Leave out speculative causes that imply the team changed something or got the protocol wrong, unless evidence supports them.
- State expectations in the present ("We expect") and as the author held them before the results. Keep numbers from the results out of predictions, and save "as expected" for a size that was actually predicted.
- Cut repetition and words that add nothing. Keep the small words a person would keep ("the least", "in the future", "at a higher altitude"): brevity stops where the sentence starts to sound compressed.
- Use the everyday word ("photos") and avoid words that collide with labels in the document ("Numbers in brackets", not "Figures in brackets"). Give each thing one name, define it once, and name it by what the reader means ("both sites", not "both transects").
- Define only what the intended reader may not know. Defining a term the reader knows talks down to them: a committee knows overdispersion, yet a short gloss of a confidence interval was welcome.
- Every phrase needs one obvious meaning. If a reader could ask "what do you mean by this?", replace it with the specific thing.
- Give concrete numbers. Write ratios above 1 by choosing the reference group, and tie each p value to its comparison.
- Claim only plans, numbers and actions the author has endorsed.

## Weigh the evidence

Explicit feedback outranks inferred patterns, and later feedback outranks earlier guidance. A repeated comment is stronger than a single one, and an edit accepted without comment is weak evidence. One correction is not a ban: apply a rule where its reason applies. The ledger marks each rule A (explicit and repeated), B (explicit once) or C (inferred).

When a claim is doubtful or ambiguous, keep its wording during a style edit and flag it. During substantive work, ask for the missing fact or leave a clear placeholder. Leave field details, dates and plans to the author, and flag any you cannot verify.

## Deliver

For a draft or rewrite, start with the prose. For a review, lead with the findings that matter most and show the revised wording. In correspondence, keep the ordinary courtesies. In instructions and agent replies, prefer affirmative guidance over hard prohibitions.

Before delivery, check that every claim keeps its meaning and strength, each sentence earns its place, and the prose sounds like a specific person rather than a template. Search once for em dashes and prose semicolons.
