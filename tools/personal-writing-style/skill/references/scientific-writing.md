# Scientific and academic writing

Use this layer for manuscripts, theses, proposals, progress reports, abstracts, captions and responses to reviewers. Rule numbers point to [comment-ledger.md](comment-ledger.md). The examples are invented.

## Protect the science

Preserve every number, citation, taxon, direction of effect, comparison and scope condition. Keep planned work grammatically distinct from completed work. When the source is ambiguous or conflicts with the implementation, keep the uncertainty and flag it. Accuracy comes before fluency.

Use stable technical terms consistently. Repetition helps when a synonym could imply a different variable, group, dataset or method. Passive voice works when the actor is irrelevant or the discipline expects it.

Hedge where the evidence is uncertain and nowhere else. A caveat that changes how the reader should use a result stays: few survey mornings, a correction for many tests, names not yet validated. A caveat that only protects the writer goes (R3). In a document for reviewers or a committee, the user wants settled work stated with confidence.

## Know the reader

Use the field's ordinary nouns. Keep established ecological terms such as "phenology". Explain a machine-learning, statistical or computational term through its role in the study when an ecologist may not know it. Leave out definitions the reader knows: expert reviewers know what overdispersion is (R12).

Name things by what the reader can picture. When a study has two kinds of sampling, introduce them once, with one name each, and keep those names (R10). Name the site when the site is meant.

Separate stable scientific names from internal development labels. In the text, use the publication name or a functional description. Keep experiment names in provenance records and reproducibility mappings.

Name quantities precisely. A total count, the number of species and which species occur are distinct outcomes.

## Report results so they read at once

- Choose the reference group so that ratios are above 1, and give the interval as exact reciprocals of the unrounded estimates (R16).
- Make words match numbers: 0.53 is "just over half", not "almost half".
- Say "not significant" and give the p value, rather than "the interval includes 1". Tie each p value to its comparison (R17).
- Name the test or correction ("the Benjamini–Hochberg correction"). When the method may be new to the author, explain it in the chat reply so the author can defend it.
- Give medians or means beside ratios when readers would want to know how many (R15).
- Describe a sampling design with its counts ("eight shoreline points and four reed-bed points per lake") rather than in abstract terms.

## Expectations and hypotheses

Write expectations in the present ("We expect", Spanish "Se espera que"). State them as the author held them before the analysis. A predicted size that happens to match the result looks written afterwards, so leave sizes out unless they were predicted (R5).

## Methods

Write Methods so another researcher can understand and reproduce the work. Name the main tools (the main software, the main model, the statistics package) and leave helper steps to an appendix when they make a sentence hard to digest (R25). Give the reason for each non-obvious design step: why a second site exists, why memory cards are swapped each week, why some days were excluded (R18). Where expert readers will check the analysis, say where the full model specification can be found.

Describe the final procedure rather than the experiments that produced it. Keep unfinished work grammatically planned, and claim only plans the author has made (R20).

## Figures

The user often asks for a figure where the text struggles: sampling coverage, the size of an effect, what changed between two setups (R24). Make each figure carry as much as it can: label what was excluded, grey out what lies outside the study area, keep legends clear of the data, use a) and b) for panels, and share axes unless a log scale is needed and explained. When a figure has internal labels, the caption explains what each one shows.

## Citations

Cite every factual claim. Put the citations once, at the end of the sentence, and use the plain form ("According to an estimate by C. Author (Author D et al. 2015)", not "cited by"). Cite studies from the field site when they are relevant, and only then (R22).

## Structure and provenance

Build each paragraph around one developing point. Keep objective-specific methods and results with their objective. When a sentence depends on an earlier detail, restate the detail briefly rather than pointing to a section (R11).

Provenance belongs in records, ledgers and source notes. In reader-facing text, mention a source only when the reader needs it to judge the claim. Notes on where a photo came from, which tool reviewed what, or which document defined a contrast are usually not for the reader (R1). What to disclose is the author's decision, and a venue's disclosure rules take precedence.

Treat prose editing and scientific verification as separate passes. Preserve values and citations during the prose pass, then flag any item that needs checking.
