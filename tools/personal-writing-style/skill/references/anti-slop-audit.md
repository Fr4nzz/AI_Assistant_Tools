# Anti-slop audit

Run this pass after the meaning and structure are sound. Judge each pattern by what it does in context. Apart from the punctuation preferences and the factual safeguards, no single word or construction makes a sentence bad.

The question behind every item is the user's own: would a person have written this? The rule numbers point to [comment-ledger.md](comment-ledger.md). The quoted examples are invented.

## Tells the user caught in their own documents

These came up repeatedly in the user's comments on agent-written reports. Check them first.

- **Sentences about the text (R1).** "Each objective is listed with its expectation, and section 5 answers each one." "The main contrasts were set out in the study plan." "The tool of step 3." "Photos from the public archive." Delete them, or keep only the heading.
- **The obvious (R2).** "There are records for every month." A limitation every reader already sees, or a caveat repeated after the reader already knows it. Delete. Keep a qualification that tells the reader a result's status (names not yet validated, an analysis that is exploratory) the first time it matters, and flag rather than delete the author's hypotheses or results.
- **Defensive hedging (R3).** "According to the protocol", "pending confirmation", "exploratory" attached to a planned main result, "not from the study site". State the fact, or give the reason in positive form ("since no expert-labelled photos exist yet, …").
- **Blame and self-damage (R4).** Remarks that the site lacks something, hypotheses that imply the team did not know its own protocol. Remove them, or replace them with what the evidence shows.
- **Hindsight predictions (R5).** A prediction with a number that matches the result, or "as expected" after a result whose size nobody predicted. An agent that has read the results tends to write these. Keep the expectation the author actually held.
- **Telegraphic compression (R8).** "Disturbs the flowers least", "so in future", "higher" where "at a higher altitude" reads naturally. Restore those small words, and keep cutting repetition that adds nothing (R25).
- **Drifting names (R10).** A place name shortened ("the Upper"), equipment named where the place is meant ("both transects"), or a label used before it is defined.
- **Unexplained shorthand (R13).** "Comparable survey series", "different field times", "the cause". Name the specific thing.
- **Qualifiers that invent a contrast (R14).** "On several continents", "long-term evidence". Ask what the qualifier implies about the cases it leaves out.
- **Unreadable numbers (R16, R17).** Ratios below 1, "the intervals include 1", a pair of p values without their comparisons, "almost half" for 0.53.
- **Unendorsed claims (R20, R21).** A sample size the author never chose, an "improved model" with no plan behind it, "we are checking with the team" when nobody has asked, "left for the thesis".

## Check what the sentence actually says

- **Inflated significance:** Replace unsupported praise such as "pivotal", "important" or "robust" with the observation or comparison behind it. Keep a word with a precise technical meaning.
- **Vague authority:** Name the source and the claim it supports.
- **Trailing participles:** A clause beginning "highlighting", "showing" or "reflecting" that only repeats the sentence can go.
- **Staged contrast:** State the point directly when "not only X but Y" merely delays it. Keep a contrast that protects a real distinction.
- **Synonym cycling:** Repeat the stable noun when a new label would blur the taxon, method or variable.
- **Ceremonial verbs:** Prefer "is" or the specific action to "serves as" or "stands as".
- **False ranges:** List the items when "from X to Y" joins things that form no scale.
- **Hidden actors:** Name the subject when passive wording hides responsibility. Keep the passive when the actor is irrelevant.
- **Changelog prose:** "Now", "newly" and "was added" expose drafting history. Keep history only in genres that need it.
- **Announced structure:** Begin the explanation instead of previewing it. After a heading, continue the argument.
- **Mechanical formatting:** Bold, symbols and title-case headings only where they change how the passage is read. A summary of findings reads better as bullets than as a table.
- **Generic completion:** End on the last substantive claim, not on importance, balance or future promise.
- **Chatbot ceremony:** In documents and direct answers, begin with the content and stop when it is complete. In real correspondence, keep the courtesies.

## Keep the voice that is already there

Preserve the author's judgments, emphasis and real uncertainty where the genre allows them. Keep unusual details that carry information. Draw personality from the source rather than adding anecdotes, jokes or opinions the author did not give.

Scientific prose can be restrained and still sound human. Its voice comes from the distinctions it protects, the explanations it chooses to give and the confidence with which it states what the evidence shows.

## Read the passage, not a list of suspect words

Look for clusters: uniform cadence, repeated transitions, serial triplets, symmetrical contrasts, abstract nouns with weak verbs, identical paragraph shapes. Repair the structure rather than swapping one word for another. Leave clear prose alone.

Finish by searching for em dashes and prose semicolons and revising each sentence that has one. En dashes for ranges and semicolons between citations stay.
