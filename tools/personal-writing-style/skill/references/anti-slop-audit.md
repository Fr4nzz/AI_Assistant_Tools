# Anti-slop audit

Use this pass after the meaning and structure are sound. Judge each pattern by what it does in context. Apart from the user's explicit punctuation preferences and factual safeguards, no isolated word or construction makes a sentence bad.

## Check what the sentence actually says

- **Inflated significance:** Replace unsupported praise such as “pivotal”, “important”, or “robust” with the observation, comparison, or consequence that supports the judgment. Keep the word when it has a precise technical meaning.
- **Vague authority:** Name the source and the claim it supports. If the source is unavailable, narrow the attribution to what the evidence can support.
- **Superficial analysis:** Check whether a trailing phrase beginning with “highlighting”, “showing”, “ensuring”, or “reflecting” adds a real action or relationship. If it only repeats the sentence, end on the substantive claim.
- **Staged contrast:** State the point directly when frames such as “not only X but Y” or “the aim is not X, it is Y” merely delay it. Keep a contrast that protects a real distinction.
- **Synonym cycling:** Repeat the stable noun when a new label would blur the taxon, method, variable, or component.
- **Elaborate substitutes for simple verbs:** Prefer “is”, “has”, or the specific action when phrases such as “serves as”, “stands as”, or “functions as” add ceremony rather than meaning.
- **False ranges:** List the subjects directly when a “from X to Y” construction joins items that do not form a real scale, sequence, or span.
- **Hidden actors:** Name the subject when passive wording or a fragment makes responsibility unclear. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or less important than the object.
- **Changelog prose:** Describe the present work when words such as “now”, “newly”, or “was added” expose an irrelevant drafting history. Retain the history in genres that need it.
- **Announced structure:** Begin the explanation instead of opening with “let's explore”, “here is what you need to know”, or another preview that adds no orientation. After a heading, continue the argument rather than restating the heading in a warm-up sentence.
- **Mechanical formatting:** Use emphasis where it changes how the passage is read. Reduce repeated bold labels, decorative symbols, and title-case headings that make ordinary information look packaged.
- **Generic completion:** End on the last substantive claim rather than announcing importance, balance, or future promise.
- **Chatbot ceremony:** In finished documents and direct answers, begin with the requested content and stop when it is complete. In real correspondence, keep courtesies that serve the relationship or invite a necessary reply.

## Keep the voice that is already there

Vary sentence length when the change supports emphasis. Preserve genuine uncertainty, irritation, preference, and first-person judgment where the genre allows them. Keep unusual details that carry information. Draw personality from the source rather than adding anecdotes, jokes, opinions, or emotional reactions that the author did not provide.

Scientific prose may be restrained and still sound human. Its voice comes from the distinctions it protects, the explanations it considers necessary, the way it handles uncertainty, and the places where it applies emphasis.

## Read the passage, not a list of suspect words

Look for clusters of habits: uniform cadence, repeated transitions, serial triplets, symmetrical contrasts, abstract nouns with weak verbs, identical paragraph shapes, or several sentences that announce rather than state. Repair the underlying structure instead of trading one suspicious word for another.

Leave clear prose alone. One polished transition, parenthetical phrase, technical passive, or repeated scientific term is not a defect by itself.

Finish by checking for em dashes and semicolons, then revise each sentence that contains one. Preserve en dashes used for ranges or established compounds.
